# What is a logic model?

A logic model is a one-page picture of how a program is supposed to work: the resources you put in, the things you do, what that produces, and the changes you expect as a result. Non-profits, foundations, schools and public health programs use them to plan a program, explain it to funders, and decide what to measure.

## What are the parts of a logic model?

Most logic models have five parts, read from left to right:

- **Inputs** (or resources): what the program needs. People, money, buildings, equipment, partners.
- **Activities:** what the program does with them. Workshops, services, outreach, training.
- **Outputs:** the direct products of the activities, usually counts. Sessions held, people served, packs handed out.
- **Outcomes:** the changes for the people or community you serve. New skills, different habits, better health. Many models split them into short-term, medium-term and long-term.
- **Impact:** the lasting change you're working toward, often in a whole community or system.

The first two describe your planned work. The last three describe the results you intend. The W.K. Kellogg Foundation's guide, still one of the most used, puts it the same way.

## What's the difference between outputs and outcomes?

This is where most logic models go wrong. **Outputs** count what you did: "40 students tutored this term". **Outcomes** describe what changed: "30 of them now read at grade level".

A quick test: could the card be true even if nobody's life changed? Then it's an output. Funders usually care about both, but they fund the outcomes.

## How do I read a logic model?

As a chain of "if… then…" statements:

> **If** we have volunteer tutors and a room, **then** we can run tutoring twice a week. **If** we run it, **then** 40 students get tutored each term. **If** they're tutored, **then** they read better. **If** children read better, **then** more of them finish school.

Every "then" rests on an assumption. Writing the assumptions down, and the evidence for them, is what turns a logic model from a diagram into a plan you can test.

## How is a logic model different from a theory of change?

They're related, and people often use the words for the same thing. The difference is in the detail:

- **A logic model** shows *what* the program does and produces, step by step, in a fixed set of columns. It's good for planning, managing and measuring one program.
- **A theory of change** explains *why* the change should happen. It usually starts from the long-term goal, works backwards to the conditions needed to get there, and spells out the assumptions and outside factors along the way. It can cover several programs or a whole organization.

Many organizations write a theory of change first, then a logic model for each program that serves it.

## How do I build a good one?

- **Start from the impact and work backwards.** Decide the change you want, then the outcomes that would show you're getting there, then what you'd need to do.
- **Keep it to one page.** A handful of items in each column. Detail belongs in your project plan.
- **Make outcomes measurable.** "Families eat better" is hard to check; "families get food every week" is not.
- **Write down your assumptions,** especially the weak links between columns.
- **Build it with the people involved:** staff, volunteers, and the people you serve. CDC's evaluation guidance recommends involving them from the start.
- **Update it.** A logic model is a working document. When you learn that a step doesn't work, change it.
