Most projects, whatever their size, go through the same broad stages: deciding to do it, planning it, doing it while keeping an eye on it, and wrapping it up. Naming these phases helps a team see where it is, and what's easy to skip.

Two things often get called "phases": the management activities that run through every project (below), and the stages of the work itself, like design, build and launch (see [Do the phases have to happen in order?](#do-the-phases-have-to-happen-in-order)).

## What are the phases of a project?

A common way to describe them, close to the process groups in the Project Management Institute's *PMBOK Guide*. They overlap more than a numbered list suggests: you keep planning as you learn, and monitoring runs the whole time.

1. **Initiating:** decide why the project is worth doing, what success looks like, and who's involved. The output is often a short brief.
2. **Planning:** work out what needs to happen, in what order, by when, and by whom. Break the work into pieces and spot the risks.
3. **Executing:** do the work.
4. **Monitoring:** compare progress with the plan while you go, and adjust. This runs alongside executing, not after it.
5. **Closing:** deliver the result, check it with the people it's for, and capture what you learned for next time.

Teams under pressure tend to jump straight to executing. The cost shows up later: work that doesn't add up to anything, or a result nobody asked for. A little time in initiating and planning, even an hour, saves a lot of rework.

## Do the phases have to happen in order?

The management activities above always overlap. The question is how the work itself is staged. There are two broad approaches:

- **Plan-driven (often called waterfall):** the work moves through stages in sequence, for example design, then build, then test, then launch. Each stage finishes before the next starts, and the plan is set early, while planning and monitoring carry on throughout. It suits work where the steps depend on each other and change is expensive: building work, events, regulated processes, fixed-price contracts.
- **Iterative:** the team works in small loops, planning a little, delivering a little and learning as it goes, many times over. This is how [agile](/knowledge-base/agile/) methods like [Scrum](/knowledge-base/scrum/) work. It suits work where you'll learn what's needed along the way.

Many projects mix them: a fixed overall plan with a milestone for each stage, and iterative work inside each stage.

## Where do milestones and timelines fit?

A **milestone** marks a point in the project: the end of a phase, or a result that matters, like "Site agreed" or "First version live". Milestones turn a long project into steps you can check off, and give everyone shared dates to work toward.

A **timeline**, often drawn as a Gantt chart, shows the work as bars across a calendar, so you can see what overlaps and what depends on what. The chart is named after Henry Gantt, who used bar charts to plan and track production in the 1910s.

When one step can't start until another finishes, that's a **dependency**. The longest chain of dependent steps sets the earliest the project can finish, so delays there delay everything.

## Doing it in Leantime

- **Initiating:** the **Project Brief** and **Project Value Canvas** [blueprints](/using-leantime/blueprints/) help you agree on the why before the work starts.
- **Planning:** set up [milestones](/using-leantime/roadmap-planning/) for each phase or result, and break them into [to-dos](/using-leantime/task-management/). A milestone can depend on another milestone, and the timeline draws the arrow between them.
- **Monitoring:** the project's [Reports](/using-leantime/reports/) show progress against the milestones, and [goals](/using-leantime/goals/) show whether the work moves the result.
- **Closing:** a [retrospective](/using-leantime/retrospectives/) captures what to continue, stop and start next time.
