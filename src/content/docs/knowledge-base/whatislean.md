Lean is a way of working that asks one question again and again: what does the customer actually value, and what are we doing that doesn't add to it? Everything else, from how work flows to how teams improve, follows from that question.

## Where does Lean come from?

Lean grew out of the Toyota Production System, developed at Toyota from the late 1940s onward, with Taiichi Ohno as its best-known architect. The word "lean" came later: researchers James Womack, Daniel Jones and Daniel Roos used it in *The Machine That Changed the World* (1990) to describe how Toyota made more with less. In *Lean Thinking* (1996), Womack and Jones turned it into five principles that could be used outside car factories.

Since then, Lean has spread to hospitals, offices, software and startups.

## What are the five principles of Lean?

From Womack and Jones, as summarized by the [Lean Enterprise Institute](https://www.lean.org/lexicon-terms/lean-thinking-and-practice/):

1. **Value:** decide what's valuable from the point of view of the end customer.
2. **Value stream:** map every step it takes to deliver that value, and remove the steps that don't add any.
3. **Flow:** make the remaining steps happen smoothly, one after another, without waiting.
4. **Pull:** start work when it's needed, not just because there's capacity to start it.
5. **Perfection:** repeat the cycle. Each round uncovers more waste to remove.

## What counts as waste?

In Lean, waste is anything that takes time or effort without adding value for the customer. Toyota named seven kinds; an eighth, unused talent, was added later. In project work they look like this:

| Waste | In a project, for example |
| --- | --- |
| **Overproduction** | Writing a 40-page plan when a one-page brief would do |
| **Waiting** | Work sits until someone approves it |
| **Transport** | Handing work from person to person, team to team |
| **Over-processing** | Polishing something nobody asked to be polished |
| **Inventory** | Half-finished work piling up |
| **Motion** | Hunting for files, switching between tools |
| **Defects** | Mistakes that have to be fixed and redone |
| **Unused talent** | Not asking the people closest to the work how to do it better |

## What is the Lean Startup?

Eric Ries applied Lean thinking to new products in *The Lean Startup* (2011). When you don't yet know what customers want, the biggest waste is building something nobody needs. So instead of a big launch, you work in a [build–measure–learn loop](https://theleanstartup.com/principles):

1. **Build** the smallest version that can teach you something, a minimum viable product.
2. **Measure** how people respond.
3. **Learn** whether to keep going or change direction (pivot).

Ash Maurya's [Lean Canvas](https://leanstack.com/lean-canvas) puts the same idea on one page: the problem, the customer, the solution and how you'll know it works, written down so you can test it and revise it as you learn.

## Does Lean work for knowledge work?

Yes, but it needs translating. Factory work repeats; projects, research and creative work rarely do. Two things matter more outside the factory:

- **Small batches and fast feedback** do more than detailed upfront plans, because the plan changes as you learn.
- **Everyone needs to understand why.** Changes made to people, rather than with them, tend to fail. A team that helps find the waste will help remove it.

## Doing it in Leantime

- **Value:** the [Lean Canvas and Project Value Canvas](/using-leantime/blueprints/) blueprints help you write down who the work is for and what problem it solves.
- **Flow and pull:** the [To-Dos Kanban board](/using-leantime/task-management/) shows where work is waiting. See [Kanban](/knowledge-base/kanban/).
- **Inventory:** the **Cumulative Flow** chart in [Reports](/using-leantime/reports/) shows when work piles up faster than it gets done.
- **Perfection:** a [retrospective](/using-leantime/retrospectives/) after each stretch of work asks what to continue, stop and start.
